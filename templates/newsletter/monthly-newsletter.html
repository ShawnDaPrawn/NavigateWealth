A note from the founder

Steady hands in a noisy year

Most of what moved your wealth this year happened slowly, and out of the headlines. That is worth remembering as we close the books on 2026.

Every December I read back through the year's notes from client meetings. What stands out is rarely the market. It is the conversations: a retirement date brought forward, a first home, a business sold, a parent who needed more care than anyone planned for. Those are the moments a financial plan is built to carry.

Markets gave us plenty to talk about, and you will find our review on the next page. But the honest summary is that patient, diversified portfolios did what they were designed to do. The investors who fared best were not the ones who predicted the year; they were the ones who did not need to.

This issue's cover story looks ahead instead of back. The twelve weeks to the end of February are the most useful in the planning calendar: allowances reset, tax-year contributions close, and there is time to act without rushing. We have set out what to check and when, so nothing is left to the last week.

Thank you for the trust you have placed in us this year. Our office closes over the festive season and reopens in the new year; if anything changes for you in the meantime, please tell us. A plan is only as good as what it knows about you.

Shawn Francisco
Founder, Navigate Wealth

Cover story · Year-end planning

Before the year turns: a twelve-week planning window

The tax year closes at the end of February. Between now and then, a few unhurried decisions can lower this year's tax, use allowances that will not roll over and leave your plan in better shape for the next one.

By the Navigate Wealth planning team 7 minute read

Year-end planning has a reputation for being complicated. Mostly, it is not. It comes down to a short list of allowances that reset on 1 March, a few contributions that only count if they land before the tax year closes, and a review of whether the plan you made still fits the life you are living.

What makes it hard is timing. December disappears into the holidays, January is busy, and by late February everyone is trying to do the same thing at once. Product providers need time to process contributions, and a payment made on the last day may not be allocated in time to count for this tax year.

1. Use the allowances that do not roll over

Your annual tax-free savings allowance is use-it-or-lose-it: whatever you do not contribute by the end of February is gone for good, and over-contributing is penalised. If you have room left this year, a single lump sum in January is often simpler than a scramble in the final week.

The same is true of annual exclusions on capital gains. If you hold investments with sizeable unrealised gains, realising some of them before the year closes, and reinvesting, can reset your base cost and use an exclusion that would otherwise lapse.

2. Top up your retirement contributions

Contributions to a retirement annuity, pension or provident fund are deductible up to 27.5% of the greater of your remuneration or taxable income, within an annual rand cap. Many people sit below that limit simply because no one checked. Topping up is one of the few ways to reduce this year's tax while building next decade's income.

It also matters more under the two-pot system. Since September 2024, part of every new contribution goes to a savings pot you can access before retirement. A slightly higher contribution keeps your retirement pot on track if you ever do need to draw on savings.

3. Check your cover still matches your life

Life, disability and income-protection cover are usually set up for the life you had at the time. A new child, a bigger bond, a change of job or a divorce can each leave a gap, or leave you paying for cover you no longer need. Year-end is a natural point to look at the sums assured against what your family would actually need.

Check your beneficiary nominations at the same time. Retirement-fund trustees decide how death benefits are paid, but your nomination guides them, and an out-of-date nomination is one of the most common causes of delays and disputes.

“The best year-end plan is the one you finish in January, not the one you start on 27 February.” The Navigate Wealth planning team

4. Rebalance, if the year has pulled you off course

A strong year in one asset class quietly changes the risk you are taking. If shares have outrun bonds and cash, your portfolio may now carry more equity than the plan intended. Rebalancing brings it back, and doing it inside a retirement fund or tax-free account avoids triggering capital gains.

5. Look ahead to the new tax year

On 1 March your allowances reset. Setting up monthly debit orders for your tax-free savings and retirement contributions from the start of the year spreads the cost and removes the year-end rush altogether. It is the single habit we most often recommend.

None of this needs to be done alone, and not all of it will apply to you. The point of a review is to work out which two or three items matter most for your circumstances, and then to do them early.

Tear-out checklist

Your year-end to-do list

  1. Tax-free savingsContribute any unused allowance by mid-February.
  2. Retirement top-upCheck how far you are from the 27.5% deduction limit.
  3. Capital gainsConsider using this year's annual exclusion.
  4. Cover reviewCompare sums assured with today's needs.
  5. BeneficiariesConfirm nominations on every fund and policy.
  6. RebalanceBring your mix back to the plan's targets.
  7. Debit ordersSet up monthly contributions from 1 March.

You asked

Your questions, answered plainly

Send yours to info@navigatewealth.co. We answer a few each month, without names.

Q

Should I pay off my bond faster or invest the extra?

A question we hear often

Paying down your bond earns you a guaranteed, tax-free return equal to your interest rate. Investing may earn more over time, but with risk. For many people the answer is both: first use your retirement and tax-free allowances, which carry tax benefits a bond does not, then put the rest into the bond. Keep an emergency fund either way, and use an access bond so the money is not locked away.

Q

My medical aid premium is going up. Should I downgrade?

Common among clients nearing retirement

Possibly, but compare like with like. Look at the hospital cover, chronic benefits and network restrictions you would give up, not only the premium. A gap cover policy alongside a lower option can close much of the difference. Moving later in life can also bring waiting periods, so check before you switch.

Q

Is now a bad time to invest, with markets near their highs?

Asked whenever markets reach new highs

Markets spend a lot of their time near all-time highs, because over the long run they rise. Waiting for a dip often costs more than it saves. If a lump sum makes you nervous, phasing it in over a few months is a sensible middle path.

Q

How often should my plan really be reviewed?

Asked at almost every first meeting

At least once a year, and whenever your life changes: a new job, a marriage or divorce, a child, a property, an inheritance. The annual review keeps the plan honest; the life events are when it matters most.

Word of the month

Sequence risk

noun · retirement planning

The risk that poor returns arrive early in retirement, just as you start drawing an income. Two retirees with the same average return can end up in very different places depending on the order the returns come in.
